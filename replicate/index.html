Robotic Budding: From Homogeneous Cells to Diverse Morphologies in Physical Self-Reproduction

Siyuan Zhang1,2, Junyan Liu1, Wanglin He1, Hod Lipson1,†

1Creative Machines Lab, Columbia University     2Department of Robotics, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor

Paper Poster Video Workshop I Workshop II

Abstract

Physical self-reproduction requires a robot to gather matter, assemble it into a functional body, and separate the resulting offspring without sacrificing its own structure. This work introduces a homogeneous modular platform that demonstrates a budding-style reproduction process across two distinct three-dimensional morphologies. Each diamond-shaped module combines two actuated joints, onboard computation and power, and reversible magnetic docking interfaces.

A teleoperated parent robot collects scattered modules into a planar composite, folds the acquired modules into a target body, and mechanically separates from a structurally equivalent offspring. The same module design supports a three-module trilobite and a five-module seal, showing that one robotic cell can realize different body plans through module count and connection topology. The experiments establish the mechanical feasibility of collection, assembly, folding, separation, and independent locomotion, while leaving autonomous navigation, closed-loop docking, and recursive reproduction for future work.

Research Question and Contribution

Physical self-reproduction poses a stricter challenge than reconfiguration because a robot must create another functional body while preserving the parent. This project asks whether one homogeneous robotic cell can support distinct body plans and enable a parent to construct an offspring from loose modules in its environment.

The system addresses this question through robotic budding. The parent retains all of its original modules, while the modules collected from the environment form the offspring. A shared module design is evaluated in a three-module trilobite and a five-module seal, allowing morphology to vary through module count and connection topology rather than hardware redesign.

Robotic Budding Framework

Unlike robotic mitosis, in which an existing body divides its own hardware, robotic budding preserves the parent and builds the offspring entirely from modules gathered from the environment. A parent with n modules sequentially collects n loose modules, forming a temporary planar assembly of 2n modules. The acquired half then folds into a three-dimensional body and separates from the parent.

System Design

Homogeneous Robotic Cell

Each module consists of two triangular segments connected by two revolute joints. Two 22 kgf·cm servo actuators provide the range of motion required to transform a planar assembly into a three-dimensional morphology. An Arduino Nano ESP32 coordinates actuation and wireless communication, while a 7.4 V Li-Po battery supplies onboard power. Five N42 neodymium magnets at each triangular end create reversible, direction-independent docking interfaces.

One Module, Two Morphologies

The same robotic cell forms two target bodies without changing its mechanical, electrical, or docking design. The trilobite uses three modules, while the seal uses five. Their different body plans arise from module count, connection topology, and joint configuration.

Teleoperated Collection and Assembly

Control Architecture

The current experiments use direct human teleoperation. A browser interface sends forward, left, and right commands to a serial host, which broadcasts them wirelessly to the modules. Periodic heartbeat messages travel in the opposite direction. Docking is confirmed visually rather than through contact, current, or force feedback.

Figure 5. Browser-based teleoperation, serial host, wireless broadcast, heartbeat feedback, and robotic modules.

Locomotion Primitives

Forward, left-forward, and right-forward motion primitives allow the parent to approach each loose module before magnetic docking. The same commands also verify that a separated offspring can move independently after the budding sequence.

Figure 6. Complete forward, left-forward, and right-forward locomotion cycles of the five-module seal.

Preliminary Results

The experiments applied the same four-stage budding sequence at two module counts. The evidence is organized from the compact three-module topology to the larger five-module morphology. Together, the demonstrations evaluated whether one module design could support end-to-end collection, folding, and separation across distinct body plans.

Three-Module Trilobite

The same module hardware and budding workflow reproduced a compact three-module trilobite. This second body plan shows that the reproduction process is not restricted to one module count or connection topology.

Discussion and Future Work

What the Demonstrations Establish

Within the tested, human-directed setting, the system completed collection, planar assembly, folding, separation, and post-separation locomotion for two target morphologies. These results support the mechanical feasibility of robotic budding with one homogeneous module design. They do not yet establish autonomous operation, repeated-trial reliability, or multigenerational reproduction.

Current Limitations

Human-selected commands and visual confirmation remain part of the control loop, so the present evidence cannot be generalized to autonomous execution. Each morphology is represented by one successful end-to-end demonstration, leaving success rates and failure modes unquantified. Only first-generation offspring were tested, so recursive reproduction remains outside the current evidence.

Next Experiments

Next experiments should replace visual teleoperation with vision-based navigation and physical docking feedback, then measure collection and separation success across repeated trials. A further test should require an offspring to collect a new module set and produce the next generation. This experiment would determine whether structural equivalence also supports recursive reproductive capability.
